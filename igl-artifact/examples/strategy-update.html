Embedding experimentation in Kestria's innovation policy

One concentrated effort: making experimentation, agility and evidence a normal part of how Kestria designs and funds its innovation policy, nationally and in its fourteen regions.

Prepared byMira Halden

The bet

Why we are concentrating on the national programme

Two reasons, and they compound. The first is the size of the budget. The second, larger one, is that the national programme writes the rules that fourteen regional agencies copy.

One · Direct spend

Kestria is about to commit €6 billion to its next five-year Innovation Programme. The rules written into the Innovation Bill now will govern how that money is designed, awarded and evaluated until 2032.

Two · Leverage

The regional agencies reuse the national programme's rules, forms and evaluation criteria rather than writing their own. Change the template once and the change spreads at no extra cost.

Primary aim

Making policy experimentation routine in Kestria's science, innovation and productivity policy.

Secondary aims
Diversification
More kinds of funder and research organisation, including focused research organisations.
Agility
Room to try new policy ideas quickly, such as internal regulatory sandboxes.
Learning
Better learning beyond experiments, through linked administrative and open data.

The strategy

Three routes, pursued at once

Every conversation below sits on one of these routes. They are deliberately redundant: a law without the capability to use it produces box-ticking, capability without a law leaves with the person who built it, and neither survives a culture that treats a null result as a scandal.

Route one

Legislate it

Write an experimentation mandate, dedicated units and protected funding into the Innovation Bill and the Regional Growth Act. A law outlasts the people who championed it.

Route two

Build it

Help institutions stand the capability up. Almost none of it needs a law; it needs someone to help design it and to navigate the internal politics.

Route three

Normalise it

Change what officials believe they are allowed to do: a community of practice, training, and above all the Audit Office.

The two priorities

Where most of the effort goes

Getting our amendments into the Innovation Bill, and helping the Research Council launch an experimentation unit and design its first experiments.

Priority one · route one

Innovation Bill amendments in Parliament

We drafted an amendment package at the request of the Science and Industry Committee's chair. The science spokespeople of both main parties have confirmed they will carry it into committee stage.

What we are asking for

  1. An experimentation mandate. A framework for experimentation in how the programme's money is awarded and spent, with a clause stating that random allocation is fair. Experimentation is blocked less by law than by legal uncertainty: officials are told it isn't forbidden, but isn't explicitly allowed either.
  2. Dedicated experimentation units at the Research Council and Innovation Kestria: small teams close to both decision-makers and operations.
  3. An internal regulatory sandbox. Time-limited exemptions from specific internal rules, case by case and with safeguards, to test alternatives in real conditions.
  4. Protected funding. No less than 2% of each programme for testing and learning. The hardest ask.

Managing expectations: we are more confident of the mandate and the units than of the protected funding.

Who we have engaged

Ana Vellin Chair, Science and Industry Committee Jonas Erke Opposition science spokesperson Committee clerks and two members' offices
The upper house, a defensive effort

The upper house rarely amends technical provisions, and its debate will centre on the budget total. The realistic ask of its members is that they don't remove ours, not that they champion them.

The public argument

Our opinion piece in the national press makes the case to the people who are in the room. It is being passed around.

What happens next

Committee stage concludes in October; third reading is expected in December.

Priority two · route two

An experimentation unit at the Research Council, and its first experiments

Two tracks that reinforce each other: the amendments make the Council create the unit, and in the meantime we help them build it, since nothing in law stops them starting now.

Where it stands

  • We have met the chief executive's office and two directors.
  • We have agreed to advise on the unit's design, and on the internal politics of getting it approved.
  • At the Council's request we are scoping a first batch of experiments on managing demand: applications have doubled since 2020 while awards stayed flat.

What happens next

The Council's board decides in November.

Second order

The other conversations, top down and bottom up

From small process changes and training to culture and future law.

R1.2Reaching the regions through the Regional Growth ActEngaged

The ask

The Innovation Bill changes what the national programme does with its own money. The Regional Growth Act is what reaches the regional agencies. We have proposed provisions that encourage, fund or require them to test their own programmes.

Where it stands

The argument lands well with the minister's office, but the draft has lost ambition since spring, leaving fewer places to attach our proposals. Once the draft is published we will take amendments to Parliament, as with the Innovation Bill.

R2.2Test and learn in the government operating reviewEngaged

Why this matters

The review examines how the whole of government works, at a depth attempted perhaps once a decade, with full backing from the Prime Minister's Office. Two of its workstreams describe our agenda in the government's own words: one on innovation, one on compliance as a brake on it.

What we are pushing for

  1. Dedicated units across ministries and agencies, close to decision-makers.
  2. Protected resources for testing and learning, rather than experiments competing for scraps.
  3. An internal regulatory sandbox across government, the same clause we seek in the Innovation Bill.
R2.3Working level: chief economists at three ministriesEngaged

Why bottom-up as well as top-down

This strand builds the people inside who can carry the change, alongside our top-down work.

Getting the plumbing right

Often the binding constraint is unglamorous: an application platform that can't randomise, guidance that never mentions experiments, data no one outside can reach. Much of it can be fixed without a law or a decision above head of unit. Innovation Kestria is procuring a new grants platform this year, which makes that a live opportunity.

R2.4A unit at Innovation KestriaStalled

Where it stands

The leadership is interested in building the capability, but a hiring freeze is blocking it.

Our read

Unlikely to move unless a law compels it. That is one of the clearest arguments for the Innovation Bill amendments: where willingness exists but the machinery blocks it, a mandate unblocks it.

R2.5Evaluation rules at the TreasuryScoping

Why it is on the list

The Treasury sets the evaluation criteria that every programme has to satisfy. If the criteria neither permit nor reward experimental evidence, no amount of enthusiasm further down will produce it.

Next step

We have found an internal champion, though we don't expect immediate results.

R3.1A community of practice for policy experimentationEngaged

The aim

Officials who want to experiment are often alone in their organisations, each solving the same problems and carrying the risk by themselves. A community makes the practice visible, shareable and safer to attempt.

Where it stands

The first workshop brought together officials and researchers from nine regions. We are designing the community with the Ministry of Enterprise.

R3.2Training for the regional agenciesLanded

Where it stands

Delivered to 40 staff across nine agencies. Two have since asked for help designing a first trial, which we are scoping.

R3.3The Audit OfficeScoping

The problem, stated plainly

Officials fear the Audit Office, and that fear contaminates everything else on this page. An official who believes a pilot with an uncertain result will be written up as waste will not run one, whatever the law says.

Our hypothesis

The Office is probably more open to experimentation than officials believe. What constrains behaviour is the belief, not the position, so a public statement that experiments are legitimate could be worth more than several of the efforts above.

Next step

Not yet started, for lack of capacity on our side. We will seek a seminar invitation and more informal contact.
